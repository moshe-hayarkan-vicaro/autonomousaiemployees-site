How to hire an AI employee without wasting a quarter

A process that starts with the work rather than the tool. Two weeks of thinking, four weeks of pilot, and a boundary written down before anything is deployed.

Most AI employee purchases fail for a reason that has nothing to do with the model. They fail because the buyer started with a tool and then went looking for work to give it. The result is a deployment that demos well and does nothing, and a team that concludes the category is overhyped.

The process below is the one that works, and it is deliberately boring. It takes about two weeks of thinking before you spend anything, and it saves a quarter of confusion.

1. Start from a role, not a tool

Write down the role you are trying to fill in the same words you would use to hire a person. Not “an AI agent for operations” but “someone to process supplier invoices and chase the exceptions”. The specificity matters because it forces you to name the work, and the work is what you are actually buying.

If you cannot write the role in one sentence, you do not have a role yet. You have a hope. Go back to the job description you would post, or to the person currently doing the work badly because they have no time.

2. Count the hours before you count the money

For the role you named, estimate how many hours a week it consumes today and what share of those hours is the same thing repeated. A role that is 30 hours a week of varied judgement is a bad candidate. A role that is 30 hours a week of which 22 are the same five tasks is a good one.

This is the single most predictive number. Teams that skip it end up automating the interesting 20 percent and wondering why nothing changed.

3. Write the boundary before the brief

Every role on this site has a “what stays human” section, and that section is the design, not a disclaimer. Before you deploy anything, write down what the AI employee must never do on its own. For finance roles it is releasing payments. For support it is refunds and security answers. For sales it is sending without approval.

The boundary should be enforced by the system, not by a policy document. If the only thing stopping an AI employee from issuing a refund is an instruction in a prompt, you do not have a control, you have a suggestion.

4. Pick one metric and one guardrail

One metric that should improve, and one that must not get worse. For a support role: deflection rate up, CSAT flat or better. For an AP role: touchless invoice rate up, duplicate payments at zero. For an SEO role: briefs delivered on time up, published pages with errors at zero.

Two numbers is enough. Teams that track eight numbers track none of them, because nobody looks at a dashboard with eight lines on it.

5. Run a four-week pilot on real work

Not a demo, not a sandbox. Real tickets, real invoices, real briefs, with a human reviewing everything. Four weeks is long enough to see the failure modes and short enough that a bad decision is cheap.

During the pilot, log every correction a human makes. That log is the most valuable artefact of the whole process: it tells you whether the problem is the model, the documentation, the process, or the role definition. In our experience it is almost never the model.

6. Widen permission only after the corrections stop

The temptation after a good pilot is to remove the human review step everywhere at once. Do it one category at a time, and only for categories where the correction rate has been near zero for a month. The failure mode of premature autonomy is not a bad answer, it is a confident commitment nobody authorised.

What to ask a vendor

Five questions that separate a real deployment from a demo:

  • What does it refuse to do? A vendor with no answer has not thought about the boundary.
  • Where does the approval step live? If it is a prompt instruction rather than a system control, it is not a control.
  • What happens when it is wrong? You want a specific answer about detection and rollback, not “it learns”.
  • What does it cost at our volume? Ask for the token maths, not a per-seat price. The cost model guide shows what to expect.
  • How do we turn it off? An AI employee you cannot cleanly remove is a liability, not an asset.

The failure modes worth naming

Automating the interesting work. The role gets scoped around what is technically impressive rather than what is tedious. Nothing improves.

No documentation to ground it. The AI employee answers from imagination because there is no help centre, no access policy, no capability library. It will sound confident and be wrong.

Measuring activity instead of outcome. “It handled 4,000 tickets” is not a result. “Deflection rose 18 points with flat CSAT” is.

Removing the human too early. The pilot goes well, review is dropped, and the first serious mistake lands on a customer rather than on a reviewer.

Where to start

If you want a default: pick the role with the highest volume, the most written-down procedure, and the lowest cost of a mistake. In most companies that is internal IT support or invoice processing, not customer-facing work. Both are unglamorous and both pay back inside a quarter.
