Proposal Writer

An AI employee that turns discovery notes and a pricing sheet into a proposal that answers the customer's actual questions.

Proposal writing is a research and assembly job disguised as a writing job. The content mostly exists: past proposals, case studies, security answers, pricing rules, the notes from the discovery call. What takes the time is finding the right pieces and shaping them around what this specific customer asked for.

That is a good fit for an AI employee, with one hard constraint. Every factual claim in a proposal is a commitment, so the role must be able to show where each claim came from. A proposal writer that invents a certification or a delivery timeline is worse than no proposal writer at all. The version that works cites its sources internally, flags anything it could not verify, and leaves pricing and legal terms to a human.

What it handlesFour use cases this role is actually scoped for.

  • Proposal assembly from discovery notes

    Takes the call notes and the customer's stated requirements, matches them against your capability library, and drafts a proposal structured around their questions rather than your standard template order.

  • RFP and security questionnaire responses

    Answers repetitive questionnaires from a maintained answer library, flags questions with no existing answer, and marks anything that needs a human sign-off. Turns a two-day job into a review.

  • Case study matching

    Picks the two or three references that actually resemble this customer, by industry, size and problem, instead of the same three logos every time.

  • Redline preparation

    Compares a customer's redlined contract against your standard terms and summarises the substantive differences for legal, so the lawyer reviews a summary rather than a 40-page document.

Scope and access

What it does

  • Drafts proposals structured around the customer's stated requirements.
  • Maintains an answer library for RFPs and security questionnaires and flags gaps.
  • Matches case studies and references to the customer's profile.
  • Cites the internal source for every factual claim and marks unverified ones.
  • Summarises contract redlines against standard terms for legal review.
  • Keeps a record of what was proposed and what was won, so the library improves.

What it needs

  • A capability library: what you actually do, with evidence.
  • A pricing sheet with rules, not just numbers.
  • Read access to past proposals and won deals.
  • A human approver for anything that commits the company.

What stays humanThe boundary is the design, not a limitation.

Not delegated

  • Pricing and commercial terms. Always.
  • Legal review of any contract language.
  • Signing off on delivery commitments and timelines.
  • The final read, because a proposal is a sales document and tone matters.

Fit

Good fit if

  • You write more than a handful of proposals a month.
  • You answer the same security and RFP questions repeatedly.
  • Your capabilities are documented somewhere.

Not a fit if

  • You write two proposals a quarter. The setup cost will not pay back.
  • Your capability claims are not written down. It will invent them.
  • You need it to price deals. Pricing is a commercial decision.

Questions people ask

Can it answer security questionnaires accurately?

Yes, if you maintain the answer library and it is allowed to say 'no existing answer' rather than guessing. The failure mode to watch for is a plausible-sounding answer to a question you have never actually addressed.

Will proposals sound generic?

They will if the input is generic. Give it the discovery notes and it structures around the customer's words. Give it a template and it produces a template.

Who owns the final document?

A human, always. The role produces a draft with sources; the account executive owns what goes out.
