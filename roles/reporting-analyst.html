Reporting Analyst

An AI employee that assembles the weekly and monthly packs, writes the commentary, and flags what changed before anyone asks.

Reporting is the job everyone has and nobody owns. The numbers get pulled, pasted into a template, formatted, and then someone writes three sentences of commentary at 11pm the night before the meeting. The commentary is the only part anyone reads, and it is the part done last and worst.

An AI employee is well suited to this because the work is assembly plus prose, both of which it does reliably. The important scoping is that it must not be the source of the numbers. It reads from the systems that own them, and its job is the pack, the commentary and the flagging. When a number looks wrong, it says so rather than formatting it neatly and moving on.

What it handlesFour use cases this role is actually scoped for.

  • Weekly and monthly pack assembly

    Pulls the numbers from source systems, fills the template, and produces the pack on schedule without anyone remembering to do it. The formatting is consistent because it is generated, not assembled by hand.

  • Variance commentary

    Writes the explanation of what moved and why, grounded in the underlying data and last period's notes. Flags variances it cannot explain rather than inventing a reason.

  • Stakeholder-specific summaries

    Produces a one-paragraph version for the executive, a detailed version for the operator, and a numbers-only version for finance, from the same source. Same truth, different length.

  • Anomaly flagging

    Compares this period against the expected range and flags anything outside it before the pack goes out, with the likely cause and what to check.

Scope and access

What it does

  • Assembles recurring reports from source systems on a schedule.
  • Writes variance commentary grounded in the data and prior notes.
  • Produces stakeholder-specific versions of the same report.
  • Flags anomalies and unexplained variances before publication.
  • Maintains the report templates and the metric definitions behind them.
  • Keeps an archive so any past number can be traced to its source.

What it needs

  • Read access to the source systems, not a manually maintained spreadsheet.
  • A report template and a definition of each metric.
  • A distribution list and a schedule.
  • A human reviewer for anything going outside the company.

What stays humanThe boundary is the design, not a limitation.

Not delegated

  • Interpreting what the numbers mean strategically.
  • Anything in an external or board report.
  • Deciding which metrics matter this quarter.
  • Explaining a bad number to a stakeholder in person.

Fit

Good fit if

  • You produce the same reports every week or month.
  • Assembly currently takes hours of copy-paste.
  • Your source systems are accessible programmatically.

Not a fit if

  • Your reporting is a spreadsheet maintained by hand. Fix the source first.
  • You produce reports ad hoc with no recurring shape.
  • You need strategic interpretation, which is a person's job.

Questions people ask

Will the commentary be generic?

It will be if it only sees the numbers. Give it last period's commentary and the decisions that were made, and it writes something with context. The quality of the commentary tracks the quality of the context you give it.

What if a number is wrong?

It should flag rather than format. The design rule is that an unexplained variance becomes a question in the pack, not a confident sentence. That is the difference between a useful report and a dangerous one.

Does it replace a finance analyst?

It replaces the assembly, which is the tedious half. The analyst keeps the interpretation and the conversations, which is the half that requires knowing the business.
