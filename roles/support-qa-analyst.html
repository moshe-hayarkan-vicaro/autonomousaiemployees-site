Customer Support

Support QA Analyst

An AI employee that reads every conversation instead of a sample, scores it against your rubric, and reports the patterns nobody had time to find.

Support QA is normally done on a sample of two or three conversations per agent per week, which means the score is mostly noise and everyone knows it. The reason is capacity, not methodology. Nobody can read a thousand conversations.

An AI employee can read all of them. That changes the job from sampling to census, which makes the numbers trustworthy and, more usefully, makes root-cause analysis possible. Instead of 'quality is 82 percent', you get 'quality is 82 percent and 40 percent of the deductions come from one broken macro on billing questions'. The second sentence is the one that gets fixed.

What it handlesFour use cases this role is actually scoped for.

  • Full-coverage conversation QA

    Scores every conversation against your rubric: resolution, tone, accuracy, policy adherence, effort. Reports distributions rather than averages, because averages hide the tail that matters.

  • Root-cause clustering

    Groups the deductions by cause rather than by agent: a wrong macro, a missing doc, a policy nobody understands, a product bug. Turns a quality score into a fix list.

  • Agent coaching notes

    Writes a short, specific note per agent per week with two examples and one thing to change. Specific enough to act on, short enough to read.

  • Escalation and risk detection

    Flags conversations containing legal threats, security disclosures, regulatory language or churn signals, so risk surfaces from the transcript rather than from a complaint later.

Scope and access

What it does

  • Scores every support conversation against a defined rubric.
  • Clusters quality deductions by root cause rather than by person.
  • Writes weekly per-agent coaching notes with concrete examples.
  • Detects legal, security and churn risk language across all conversations.
  • Tracks whether a fix actually improved the score, by comparing before and after.
  • Maintains the rubric itself, flagging criteria that never fire or always fire.

What it needs

  • Read access to the full conversation history, not a sample.
  • A written rubric. Without one it will invent criteria you did not agree to.
  • A definition of what counts as a good outcome, which is a management decision.
  • A destination for the reports: a channel, a dashboard, or a weekly doc.

What stays humanThe boundary is the design, not a limitation.

Not delegated

  • Setting and changing the rubric. This is where the values of the team live.
  • Any disciplinary use of the scores. A model's judgement should not be the sole basis for a performance decision.
  • Acting on the root causes it finds.
  • Judging edge cases where policy and kindness conflict.

Fit

Good fit if

  • You have enough conversation volume that sampling feels dishonest.
  • You have a QA rubric, even an informal one.
  • Someone acts on quality findings.

Not a fit if

  • You have five conversations a day. Read them yourself.
  • You have no rubric and no intention of writing one.
  • You want to use it to justify firing people. That is a misuse and it will not hold up.

Questions people ask

Can it judge tone reliably?

Reasonably, on clear cases, and unreliably on subtle ones. Use it for distribution and pattern detection, and have a human review anything that would affect a person's record.

Does it replace a QA lead?

No. It replaces the reading. The QA lead still decides what good looks like, which is the part that requires judgement about your customers.

What about privacy?

It reads customer conversations, so it needs the same data handling as your helpdesk. Redact what you would redact for a human reviewer, and keep the retention policy the same.
