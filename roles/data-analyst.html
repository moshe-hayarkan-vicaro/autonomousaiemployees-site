Data & Analytics

Data Analyst

An AI employee that answers the question behind the question, writes the query, and shows its work so the number can be checked.

Most analytics teams do not have a dashboard problem, they have a question-answering problem. Someone asks why a number moved, and a person spends two days pulling data to write four paragraphs. The dashboard existed the whole time and nobody looked at it, because the question was specific and the dashboard was general.

An AI data analyst fills that gap, with one non-negotiable rule: it must show its work. Every figure it states should come with the query that produced it, so a human can re-run it and check. A number without a query is an opinion. The role is scoped to answering questions and writing prose about data, never to being the system of record for anything.

What it handlesFour use cases this role is actually scoped for.

  • Ad-hoc question answering

    Takes a business question in plain language, writes the query, runs it, and answers in prose with the query attached. Turns a two-day request into a twenty-minute one, and the analyst reviews rather than builds.

  • Metric movement investigation

    When a metric moves, checks the usual causes in order: seasonality, a tracking change, a mix shift, a genuine change in behaviour. Reports which one the evidence supports and which it rules out.

  • Recurring report automation

    Builds the weekly and monthly reports once, then regenerates them with fresh numbers and a written summary of what changed. The summary is the part humans actually read.

  • Data quality flagging

    Notices when a table stops updating, a column fills with nulls, or a metric jumps in a way that suggests a pipeline problem rather than a business change, and says so before the number reaches a slide.

Scope and access

What it does

  • Translates business questions into queries and answers them in prose.
  • Attaches the query and the assumptions to every number it reports.
  • Investigates metric movements and rules causes in or out.
  • Regenerates recurring reports with a written summary of changes.
  • Flags data quality problems before they reach a decision.
  • Maintains a metric dictionary so definitions stop drifting between teams.

What it needs

  • Read access to the warehouse, ideally through a governed semantic layer.
  • A metric dictionary, or it will invent definitions that disagree with finance.
  • A place to deliver answers: a channel, a notebook, or a doc.
  • A rule that it never writes to production tables.

What stays humanThe boundary is the design, not a limitation.

Not delegated

  • Deciding what the numbers mean for the business.
  • Any number that goes into a board deck or an external report.
  • Defining metrics in the first place, which is a political act.
  • Access control on sensitive data.

How to measure itIf you cannot measure it, you cannot tell whether it is working.

MetricHow to read it
Questions answered per weekVolume of ad-hoc requests resolved without an analyst building from scratch.
Time to answerMedian hours from question to answer. The number the business feels.

Fit

Good fit if

  • You have a warehouse with reasonable documentation.
  • Analysts spend most of their time on ad-hoc requests.
  • Someone reviews numbers before they are published.

Not a fit if

  • Your data lives in spreadsheets on individual laptops.
  • You have no metric definitions, so every answer will be arguable.
  • You want it to write to production tables. Do not.

Questions people ask

Can it be trusted with numbers?

Only if it shows the query. The design rule is that every figure is reproducible by a human in under a minute. Without that, a plausible wrong number is worse than no number, because it gets acted on.

Does it replace an analyst?

It replaces the ad-hoc queue, which is the part analysts resent. The analyst moves to modelling, definitions and the questions that need judgement about what to measure.

What about sensitive data?

It needs the same access controls as a human analyst, and ideally a governed layer that enforces row-level permissions. Do not give it a superuser connection because it is convenient.
