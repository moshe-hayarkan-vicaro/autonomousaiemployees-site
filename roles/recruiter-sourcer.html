People & Recruiting

Recruiter / Sourcer

An AI employee that builds the pipeline, writes the outreach, and keeps candidates informed, while a person makes every hiring decision.

Recruiting is a judgement business wrapped in an administration business. The administration part, sourcing lists, outreach, scheduling, status updates, interview logistics, is where recruiters lose their week. The judgement part, who to hire, is not delegable and should not be.

Every role here is scoped to the administration side, and there is a compliance reason as well as a practical one. Screening criteria and rejection reasons are regulated in many jurisdictions, and automated decision-making about candidates carries specific legal obligations in several of them. An AI employee that touches candidate decisions needs a human in the loop by design, not by policy. The version that works keeps the model away from the decision entirely and uses it for the coordination that nobody has time for.

What it handlesFour use cases this role is actually scoped for.

  • Sourcing list building

    Builds candidate lists from stated criteria, with the evidence for each match and a note on where the information came from. Reports the criteria it could not assess rather than guessing.

  • Candidate outreach drafting

    Writes personalised first messages grounded in the candidate's actual work, and queues them for recruiter approval. The recruiter edits and sends, which is faster than writing from scratch and keeps the human voice.

  • Interview scheduling and logistics

    Coordinates availability across candidates and interviewers, books the slot, sends the calendar invite and the prep material, and chases the no-shows. The single most automatable part of recruiting.

  • Status updates and candidate care

    Keeps every candidate informed at every stage, including the rejections. Most companies are bad at this because it is tedious, and it is the thing candidates remember most.

Scope and access

What it does

  • Builds sourcing lists from defined criteria with evidence per candidate.
  • Drafts personalised outreach for recruiter approval.
  • Coordinates interview scheduling across candidates and interviewers.
  • Sends stage updates and rejection notes on a defined cadence.
  • Maintains the applicant tracking system so the pipeline is accurate.
  • Reports pipeline health: stage conversion, time in stage, drop-off points.

What it needs

  • Read access to the ATS and the calendar.
  • A written definition of the role and the must-have criteria.
  • An approval step for all candidate-facing messages.
  • A written policy on what the model may not assess, which should include anything that functions as a hiring decision.

What stays humanThe boundary is the design, not a limitation.

Not delegated

  • Every hiring decision, without exception.
  • Judging candidate quality and culture fit.
  • Rejection decisions and their reasons.
  • Offer negotiation and anything touching compensation.

Fit

Good fit if

  • You hire more than a handful of roles a year.
  • Scheduling is a genuine bottleneck.
  • You care about candidate experience and currently fail at it.

Not a fit if

  • You want it to screen and reject candidates. That is a legal exposure, not an efficiency gain.
  • You hire two people a year. The setup will not pay back.
  • Your role definitions are vague, so there is nothing to source against.

Questions people ask

Is AI screening legal?

It depends heavily on jurisdiction and on whether the output affects a decision. Several regimes require disclosure, human review and bias auditing for automated hiring tools. The safe design is to keep the model out of the decision entirely and use it for coordination, which is where the time actually goes.

Will candidates mind?

They mind being ignored, which is the status quo. A prompt, accurate status update from an AI employee is better received than silence from a human. Disclose that scheduling is automated and nobody objects.

Does it replace a recruiter?

It removes the coordination load, which is roughly half the week. The recruiter spends that time on candidates and hiring managers, which is the part that actually improves hiring.
