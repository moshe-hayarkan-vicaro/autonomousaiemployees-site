Admin & Executive

Executive Assistant

An AI employee that holds the calendar, triages the inbox, prepares the meetings and writes the follow-up before anyone has left the call.

Executive support is the role people most often imagine when they hear AI employee, and the one where expectations most often outrun reality. An AI employee cannot read a room, cannot tell that a meeting is going badly, and cannot decide that a request should be declined because the person asking is trouble.

What it can do is most of the job by hours. Hold the calendar and defend the focus blocks. Triage the inbox into what needs a decision and what needs a reply. Write the agenda, gather the context, take the notes, and produce the follow-up with the decisions and owners already captured. That is the work that eats an executive's week, and it is coordination rather than judgement, which is exactly the boundary that makes delegation safe.

What it handlesFour use cases this role is actually scoped for.

  • Calendar and scheduling

    Books meetings, resolves conflicts, protects focus blocks, and handles the multi-party scheduling that otherwise takes six messages. Declines or reschedules according to rules you set, and asks when the rules do not cover it.

  • Inbox triage

    Sorts the inbox into needs a decision, needs a reply, needs a read, and ignore. Drafts replies for the second category. The executive reviews a short list instead of a hundred messages.

  • Meeting prep and follow-up

    Sends a brief before each meeting with the context and the open items, and produces the follow-up after with decisions, owners and dates. The follow-up is the part that usually never gets written.

  • Weekly review assembly

    Assembles a weekly view of what is outstanding, what is waiting on someone else, and what has slipped, so the review is about decisions rather than reconstruction.

Scope and access

What it does

  • Manages the calendar, resolves conflicts and protects focus time.
  • Triages the inbox and drafts replies for routine messages.
  • Prepares meeting briefs with context and open items.
  • Captures decisions, owners and dates from meetings and drafts the follow-up.
  • Assembles a weekly outstanding-items review.
  • Keeps a contact and commitment log so nothing is dropped.

What it needs

  • Calendar and email access, with send permission gated by approval at first.
  • A written set of scheduling rules: what to accept, what to decline, what to ask about.
  • Access to the documents and threads relevant to upcoming meetings.
  • A clear list of people whose messages always get through.

What stays humanThe boundary is the design, not a limitation.

Not delegated

  • Relationships. The check-in, the condolence, the difficult conversation.
  • Judging priority when two urgent things collide.
  • Anything political, sensitive or confidential by nature.
  • Sending on the executive's behalf without review, at least initially.

Fit

Good fit if

  • The executive's calendar is the bottleneck for the team.
  • Scheduling and inbox are consuming hours daily.
  • You can write down scheduling rules, even roughly.

Not a fit if

  • You want it to manage relationships. It cannot.
  • The role is mostly confidential judgement calls.
  • You are not willing to write down any rules, so it will guess and guess wrong.

Questions people ask

Can it send email as me?

Technically yes. Practically, start with drafts and an approval step, and only widen send permission for categories you have watched it handle correctly for a month. The failure mode is not a rude email, it is a confident commitment you did not make.

Will it handle my calendar well?

As well as your rules are written. With no rules it will accept everything and you will be in more meetings, not fewer. The rules are the product.

Does it replace an assistant?

It replaces the coordination, which is most of the hours. A good assistant becomes more valuable because they spend their time on the things a model cannot do, which is the relationship and the judgement.
